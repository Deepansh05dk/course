<Incident title="Three weeks of 'it feels better'" severity="SEV3">
Relay's team spent three weeks tweaking prompts, chunk sizes and models. Every change was judged by an engineer trying five favourite questions and saying "feels better." After three weeks, nobody could say whether the system had improved overall, and two things that used to work (refund conditions, customs exceptions) had quietly broken. The team had been **optimizing in the dark**.
</Incident>

Every applied-AI job posting in the [research](/research/) asks for evals, and for a reason: **evals are what turn LLM work from guessing into engineering.** Without them, you can't tell whether a change helped, you can't safely upgrade a model, and you can't prove to a customer that the system works.

## Why exact asserts die

```python
# Traditional software: works forever
assert add(2, 2) == 4

# LLM software: fails on harmless rewording, passes on harmful nonsense
assert answer("What is the refund window?") == "The refund window is 30 days."
```

LLM output varies in wording, length and order, even when it's correct. So evals check **properties** ("mentions 30 days", "cites the refund policy", "doesn't promise a refund") and report **rates** ("94% of 200 cases pass"), not exact strings.

## What an eval is

An eval is three things:

1. **A dataset:** inputs (and sometimes reference outputs or labels) that represent what users actually do.
2. **A task runner:** runs your system (prompt, retrieval, agent…) on each input and records the full trace.
3. **Scorers:** functions that judge each output: code checks, LLM judges, or human review.

The output is a **scorecard** you can compare across versions: "v7: 91% pass, v8: 94% pass, but refunds dropped from 98% to 90%."

## The eval pyramid

<Diagram
	caption="Like the testing pyramid: many cheap, fast checks at the bottom; fewer expensive, high-signal ones at the top."
	code={`flowchart TB
    A["Production monitoring + A/B tests<br/>real users, real outcomes"] --> B
    B["Human review<br/>expert labels on samples, highest trust, slowest"] --> C
    C["LLM-as-judge<br/>nuanced criteria at scale, must be calibrated"] --> D
    D["Code-based checks<br/>schema, regex, exact facts, citations exist<br/>fast, cheap, deterministic"]`}
/>

| Level | Example for Relay | Cost | Runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Code checks** | Valid JSON; cited chunk IDs were retrieved; every number in the answer appears in a source; no refund promise (regex) | ~free | Every commit, every request |
| **LLM judge** | "Does the answer address the customer's actual question?" "Is it faithful to the sources?" | $ | Every PR (on the eval set); a sample of production |
| **Human review** | A policy expert grades 50 answers a week | $$$ | Weekly; to calibrate judges |
| **Online / A/B** | Edit ratio, ticket reopen rate, escalation rate | Free to measure, slow to read | Continuously |

**Push every check as low as it can go.** If code can check it, don't use a judge. If a judge can check it reliably (calibrated against humans), don't spend expert time on it.

## How evals fit into the development loop

<Diagram
	caption="The eval-driven development loop: look at data, decide what to fix, prove the fix, protect it forever."
	code={`flowchart LR
    A["Look at traces<br/>error analysis"] --> B["Name the failure<br/>taxonomy"]
    B --> C["Write eval cases<br/>+ scorer for it"]
    C --> D["Change the system"]
    D --> E["Run evals:<br/>better? anything worse?"]
    E -- "regression" --> D
    E -- "improved" --> F["Ship + keep the cases<br/>in CI forever"]
    F --> A`}

<ThinkFirst question="Your manager asks: 'We have 3 weeks. Should we spend the first week building evals, or start improving the prompts right away?' Make the case either way, then give your recommendation.">

**The case for prompts first:** visible progress, quick wins, and stakeholders want demos.

**The case for evals first:** without them, three weeks of changes can't be measured, regressions go unnoticed (as in the incident), and you can't tell the customer how good the system is.

**Recommendation: a thin eval first, in 1–2 days, not a week.**

1. Day 1: read 50–100 real traces and write down the main failure types (error analysis).
2. Day 2: turn them into 30–50 cases with simple code checks, plus one LLM judge for the most important criterion.
3. Then improve the system **against** that eval, adding cases as you discover new failures.

Evals don't have to be big to be useful. They have to exist **before** the changes they measure.

</ThinkFirst>
