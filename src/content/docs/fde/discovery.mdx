<Incident title="Success, according to whom?" severity="SEV2">
After a ten-week pilot, the engineering team presented the results: 94% answer accuracy on the eval set, a p95 latency of 3 seconds. The head of support asked: "And how much faster are my agents?" Nobody knew; handle time had never been measured before the pilot. The CFO asked: "And how much did we save?" Nobody had agreed what counted. The pilot was technically excellent and commercially unproven. The renewal was postponed.
</Incident>

Discovery answers three questions before anything is built: **which workflow**, **what would better look like (in numbers)**, and **what do we need to get there** (data, access, people). It's the highest-leverage time in the whole engagement.

## Understand the work, not the request

Customers ask for solutions ("we want a chatbot"). Your job is to find the problem underneath. The most effective method is simple: **watch people work.**

- **Shadow the workflow** for a few hours: sit with support agents, claims handlers, dispatchers. Note every system they open, every copy-paste, every lookup, every wait.
- **Follow a few cases end to end**, from the customer's first email to resolution.
- **Collect artifacts:** real tickets, documents, templates, the internal wiki, the spreadsheets people actually use.
- **Ask about exceptions:** "What's the most annoying ticket type?", "What do new hires get wrong?", "When does this go badly?"

## Questions that matter

| Area | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Volume and value** | How many of these per month? How long does each take? What does a mistake cost? |
| **Variation** | What share is routine? What are the hard cases? |
| **Data** | Where does the needed information live? Who owns it? How clean is it? Can we get access, and how long will that take? |
| **Risk** | What's the worst thing an AI mistake could cause here? Who's accountable today? |
| **People** | Who does this work? What would they welcome, and what would they resist? |
| **Measurement** | What's measured today? What would prove success to the sponsor, to users, to finance? |

## Scoring candidate use cases

Discovery usually surfaces several candidates. Score them before choosing:

| Candidate (Acme) | Value | Feasibility (data, access) | Risk if wrong | Measurability | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Draft replies for shipment-status tickets (40% of volume) | High | Medium (tracking export now, API later) | Low (agent reviews every draft) | High (handle time) | **Start here** |
| Automatic damage-claim decisions | High | Low (photos, policies, carrier data) | High (money, disputes) | Medium | Later, with human approval |
| Summaries of long customer email threads | Medium | High | Low | Medium | Quick win, bundle with the first |
| "AI that answers any question about our business" | Unclear | Low | Unclear | Low | Reframe into specific workflows |

The best first use case is usually **frequent, narrow, low-risk and measurable**, not the most impressive one. A visible early win buys the trust to tackle the harder cases.

## Baselines and success metrics

You can't show improvement without a **baseline**, measured **before** the pilot starts. Agree with the customer, in writing:

| Metric type | Example | Who cares |
|---|---|---|
| **Business outcome** (primary) | Median handle time for shipment-status tickets: 11 min → target 8.5 min | Sponsor, finance |
| **Adoption** | Share of eligible tickets where agents use the draft: target ≥ 60% | Process owner |
| **Quality guardrails** | Customer satisfaction not lower; escalation rate not higher; zero wrong refund promises | Process owner, sponsor |
| **Technical** | Faithfulness ≥ 95% on the eval set; p95 latency < 4 s | You (supporting metrics, not the headline) |

Agree the **primary metric, the target, the measurement method, and the decision rule** ("if handle time drops by at least 15% with no drop in CSAT, we roll out") before the pilot starts. Deciding afterwards invites both sides to see what they want in the numbers.

## A first ROI sketch

Discovery should end with an honest, rough business case. Not to promise a number, but to know whether the use case is worth a pilot, and which assumptions the pilot must test:

<ThinkFirst question="Acme's VP of Operations says: 'Success is when the AI handles 80% of tickets on its own.' What do you say?">

Take the ambition seriously, and turn it into something measurable and safe:

1. **Ask what's behind it:** is the goal lower cost, faster responses, handling growth without hiring? The real goal decides the metric.
2. **Explain the path:** start with AI drafts that agents approve (low risk, measurable quickly), measure quality per ticket type, and move the types that prove reliable to automatic handling. "80% autonomous" becomes the destination, not the pilot's success criterion.
3. **Propose pilot metrics** that predict that destination: handle time, adoption, the share of drafts sent without edits **per ticket type** (a measure of readiness for automation), and quality guardrails.
4. **Write it down together**, with the decision rule for each step toward automation.

You keep the VP's goal, and replace a promise you can't verify with a plan you can.

</ThinkFirst>

<Practice>

- **Warm-up:** pick a workflow you know well (at your job, or something like returning a parcel). Map every step with an estimated time. Mark the steps where AI could help.
- **Stretch:** write five discovery interview questions for Acme's claims team, and for each, what you'd do with the answer.
- **Boss level:** write a one-page discovery summary for Acme's support engagement: the workflow map, three scored use cases, the chosen one, baseline numbers, agreed metrics with targets, and an ROI sketch with its assumptions.

</Practice>
