Interview processes vary by company, but for these roles they usually combine some of: a coding round, an **LLM system design** round, a **take-home** or live build, a **customer role-play** (especially for FDE roles), and conversations about past projects. This lesson prepares you for each, using everything in the course.

## LLM system design: a framework

Interviewers want to see how you **think**: clarifying, choosing, trading off, and anticipating failure. A structure for 45 minutes:

<Diagram
	caption="Spend real time on the first and last boxes. Most candidates rush requirements and never get to failure modes, which is where senior judgment shows."
	code={`flowchart LR
    A["1. Clarify<br/>users, scale, risk,<br/>success metric"] --> B["2. Baseline<br/>simplest thing<br/>that could work"]
    B --> C["3. Architecture<br/>data, retrieval,<br/>model calls, tools"]
    C --> D["4. Quality<br/>evals, metrics,<br/>human review"]
    D --> E["5. Production<br/>cost, latency,<br/>security, ops"]
    E --> F["6. Failure modes<br/>and what you'd<br/>do about them"]`}
/>

| Step | What to say | Course modules |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Clarify** | Who uses it, how many, what's the worst mistake, how we'll know it works | 0, 12 |
| **2. Baseline** | Start with the simplest design (one call, good prompt, maybe retrieval) and say why you'd add complexity only when evals demand it | 1, 6 |
| **3. Architecture** | Data and ingestion, retrieval (hybrid, reranking, permissions), model choice, tools, workflow vs agent | 3, 4, 6, 7 |
| **4. Quality** | The eval set, how it's built, which metrics, LLM judges and their calibration, online monitoring | 5 |
| **5. Production** | Cost per request with numbers, caching, latency budget, streaming, security, observability | 8–11 |
| **6. Failure modes** | Three or four concrete ways it fails, with detection and mitigation | Every lesson's failure cards |

**Do the numbers out loud.** "40,000 tickets a month, about 3,000 input tokens each, so 120 million input tokens; with caching most of the prefix is cheap; that's roughly…" Estimation shows you've run real systems.

## A worked example

**"Design an assistant that answers employees' HR policy questions for a 20,000-person company."**

1. **Clarify:** employees in 12 countries, policies differ per country and job level; the worst mistake is a confident wrong answer about benefits or leave; success = fewer HR tickets, high answer accuracy, employees trust it.
2. **Baseline:** RAG over the policy documents with citations; no agent needed at first.
3. **Architecture:** ingestion with country and effective-date metadata; **permission-aware retrieval** (an employee sees their country's and level's policies); hybrid search and reranking; answers must cite passages; "I don't know, here's how to reach HR" when retrieval is weak; SSO.
4. **Quality:** 300 real questions from HR tickets with reference answers per country; faithfulness and correctness judges calibrated against HR experts; per-country slices; HR reviews a weekly sample.
5. **Production:** volume and cost estimate; prompt caching; streaming; PII rules (questions can contain health data: redact in logs); audit trail.
6. **Failure modes:** outdated policy versions (effective dates, superseded docs filtered); the wrong country's policy (metadata filters, eval cases per country); questions needing judgment (route to HR); prompt injection via uploaded documents (only HR can publish to the corpus).

## Take-home projects that stand out

Most take-homes get a working demo. The ones that stand out show **production thinking**:

- **An eval set and results**, even a small one, with a short error analysis.
- **Failure handling:** what happens on bad input, empty retrieval, tool errors, timeouts.
- **A README with decisions and trade-offs:** what you chose, what you didn't, and what you'd do next with more time.
- **Cost and latency numbers**, measured.
- **Scope discipline:** a smaller thing done well beats a bigger thing that half-works. Say what you deliberately left out.

## Customer role-plays

FDE interviews often include a role-play: the interviewer plays a customer (a skeptical CTO, a frustrated sponsor, an over-enthusiastic VP). They're assessing whether you listen, ask good questions, stay honest, and steer toward a plan. Practise one:

The pattern that works in every role-play: **acknowledge, ask, explain with specifics, propose a next step, and never overclaim.**

## Telling your project stories

Your capstones and labs are your best interview material. Tell each as a short story:

1. **The problem and constraints** (one sentence each).
2. **One key decision** and its trade-off ("I chose a workflow over an agent because…").
3. **Something that went wrong** and how you found and fixed it (evals, traces, a red-team test).
4. **A number** (quality before and after, cost per request, latency).

"Something that went wrong" is the part interviewers remember. This course has given you dozens of them: the canary that leaked half a prompt, the semantic cache that served another customer's shipment, the `hash()` idempotency bug. Use them.

<ThinkFirst question="An interviewer asks: 'Tell me about a time an AI system you built didn't work.' Draft a 60-second answer from one of your course labs.">

An example from the Module 10 lab:

"I added a semantic cache to a support assistant to cut costs. Before shipping, I built a small set of labeled near-miss question pairs, and the test showed that a question about a different shipment ID scored a similarity of 1.0 with a cached one: the cache would have served one customer another customer's shipment status. Negations like 'how do I NOT cancel' also scored higher than real paraphrases, so there was no safe threshold. I fixed the leak by scoping the cache by tenant and by the exact entities in the question, and in the end I mostly relied on prompt caching and exact-match caching instead, which got about the same savings without the risk. The lesson I took away: measure the failure cases before you measure the savings."

Problem, what went wrong, how it was found (a test, not a customer), the fix, and the lesson, all in about a minute.

</ThinkFirst>

<Practice>

- **Warm-up:** do the worked example above yourself in 45 minutes, out loud, with a timer. Record it and listen back.
- **Stretch:** write the "something went wrong" story for three of your labs, each under 60 seconds.
- **Boss level:** do a mock interview with a friend: one system design question (they pick from: a contract-review assistant, a sales-call summarizer, an IT helpdesk agent, a deep-research tool) and one customer role-play. Ask for blunt feedback against the framework.

</Practice>
