## Demos that build trust

A demo's job isn't to impress. It's to create **accurate expectations** in the people who'll decide and the people who'll use it.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the customer's **real** data (with permission), including messy cases | Hand-pick only the cases that work |
| Show a failure, and what happens then ("it says it isn't sure and routes to an agent") | Hide failure modes and hope |
| Show the human controls: review, edit, approve, feedback | Present it as fully autonomous when it isn't |
| Tie it to their metric ("this ticket took 11 minutes; with the draft, about 4") | Show generic AI tricks unrelated to their workflow |
| Let a user drive it live | Only drive it yourself, on a script |

Showing a failure deliberately is one of the most trust-building things you can do. It tells the audience you understand the system and won't oversell it.

## Designing a pilot that proves something

A pilot is an experiment. Design it like one:

	caption="A pilot with a control group: similar agents, same period, same ticket types. Only the AI differs, so the difference in outcomes can be attributed to it."

| Element | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** (lesson 2) | The starting point everything is compared with |
| **Control group** (same period, comparable agents) | Removes seasonal effects, policy changes, and the pilot team simply trying harder |
| **Enough volume and time** | Small samples produce noise that looks like results (see below) |
| **Pre-agreed decision rule** | Prevents arguing about the result afterwards |
| **Guardrail metrics** | A faster process that makes more mistakes isn't a win |
| **A novelty and learning period** | Week one is learning; measure the steady state, but report everything |

## Did it work? The analysis

Handle times are skewed (a few tickets take very long), so compare **medians** with a **bootstrap confidence interval** rather than averages and gut feeling. A small, tested helper:

```python
"""Did the pilot work? Compare pilot and control groups with bootstrap confidence intervals."""
import random
import statistics


def bootstrap_diff(a: list[float], b: list[float], stat=statistics.median, n: int = 5000, seed: int = 0):
    """Return the observed stat(b) - stat(a) and a 95% confidence interval for it."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    diffs = sorted(
        stat(rng.choices(b, k=len(b))) - stat(rng.choices(a, k=len(a)))
        for _ in range(n)
    )
    return stat(b) - stat(a), (diffs[int(0.025 * n)], diffs[int(0.975 * n)])


def rate_ci(successes: int, total: int, n: int = 5000, seed: int = 0):
    """A 95% bootstrap confidence interval for a rate (e.g. tickets resolved without escalation)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    outcomes = [1] * successes + [0] * (total - successes)
    rates = sorted(sum(rng.choices(outcomes, k=total)) / total for _ in range(n))
    return successes / total, (rates[int(0.025 * n)], rates[int(0.975 * n)])
```

On simulated pilot data (900 control tickets, 850 pilot tickets):

```text
full pilot (850 tickets)       median 8.6 min vs 10.4 min control | change -1.8 min, 95% CI [-2.5, -1.2]
first week only (40 tickets)   median 9.4 min vs 10.4 min control | change -1.0 min, 95% CI [-2.8, +2.7]
drafts sent with light or no edits: 72% (95% CI 69%-75%)
same metric, first week only:       72% (95% CI 57%-85%)
```

The full pilot shows a clear improvement: handle time down 1.8 minutes, and the whole 95% interval (−2.5 to −1.2) is below zero. The **same pilot after its first week** (40 tickets) shows a confidence interval from −2.8 to **+2.7** minutes: the data can't distinguish "much faster" from "slower". A first-week readout presented as "1 minute faster!" would have been noise dressed as a result. The adoption rate has the same pattern: 72% either way, but ±15 points after one week versus ±3 at the end.

Report results with their uncertainty, segment them (by ticket type, agent tenure, language), and read some of the worst cases yourself.

## Adoption is the real risk

Most AI pilots that fail commercially fail on **adoption**, not model quality. What helps:

- **Co-design with users** from the POC on: 2–3 agents who try every version and shape it. They become your champions.
- **Fit the existing workflow:** drafts appear in the helpdesk they already use, not in a new tab.
- **Fast feedback loops:** a thumbs-down with a reason, reviewed weekly, with visible fixes ("you told us X, we changed Y").
- **Respect their expertise:** position the AI as removing tedious steps, with agents in control of what's sent.
- **Measure adoption per person**, and talk to the people who stopped using it.

## Handover

An engagement isn't finished until the customer can run, change and trust the system without you.

| Handover item | Content |
|---|---|
| **Ownership** | A named team that owns the service, its budget and its on-call |
| **Runbooks** (Module 9) | For each alert: what it means, what to check, how to contain |
| **Evals and change process** | The eval suite, how to run it, and the rule that every prompt, model or retrieval change goes through it |
| **Architecture and decisions** | The design docs, the data-flow diagram, and a decision log ("why we chose X") |
| **Training** | For users (how to use it well, how to give feedback) and for the owning engineers (how it works, how to change it safely) |
| **Support path** | How they reach your company for product issues, model updates and escalations |

A good test: could the owning team handle a model migration (Module 9) on their own? If not, the handover isn't done.

<ThinkFirst question="At the go/no-go meeting, the pilot hit the handle-time target (−19%, interval −24% to −14%) but adoption was 48%, below the 60% target. The sponsor asks for your recommendation.">

Don't hide the miss, and don't reduce the decision to the headline number:

1. **Report both honestly:** the effect where it's used is strong and clearly real; adoption is below target.
2. **Explain the adoption gap with data:** adoption per agent, per ticket type, and the feedback. Often a few causes dominate (one ticket subtype with poor drafts, a few agents who never started, a workflow friction).
3. **Recommend a conditional go:** roll out to the next team with two specific fixes for the main adoption causes, a new adoption target, and a check-in date, or extend the pilot by four weeks with the fixes if the causes are unclear.
4. **Restate the ROI** at the observed adoption (the ROI sketch from lesson 2): if it still pays back, say so.

The sponsor gets an evidence-based recommendation instead of a pass/fail verdict, and the agreed criteria stay meaningful.

</ThinkFirst>

## 🧪 Module 12 lab: an FDE simulation

Do this with a friend or colleague playing the customer (they get a short brief: their business, their pain, their constraints, and a few surprises to reveal only if asked).

**Deliverables:**

1. **Discovery notes:** the workflow map with timings, scored use cases, the chosen one, and the baseline numbers.
2. **The one-page design doc**, explicitly agreed by your "customer".
3. **A data and access audit** and a day-one request email.
4. **A working POC** on realistic synthetic data, with an eval set and documented failure modes (this is where Modules 1–11 come in).
5. **A demo** to your "customer", including one deliberate failure.
6. **A simulated pilot analysis:** generate plausible pilot data, analyse it with confidence intervals, and write a go/no-go recommendation.
7. **A handover pack:** runbooks, the eval change process, and a decision log.
8. **A post-mortem** of the engagement: what you'd do differently.
