## Why a B-tree can't help

Normal database indexes (B-trees) work because data can be **sorted** along one dimension: "find prices between 10 and 20" jumps straight to the right range. Nearest-neighbour search in 384 or 1,536 dimensions has no such ordering:

- Sorting by any single dimension says almost nothing about overall closeness.
- Classic spatial indexes (k-d trees, R-trees) partition space into boxes. They work well in 2–3 dimensions (maps!), but in hundreds of dimensions nearly every box has to be checked, which degrades to scanning everything.

## Brute force: exact, simple, and linear

**Exact k-nearest-neighbours (kNN)** is easy: compute the similarity between the query and every vector, and keep the top k. With normalized vectors it's one matrix multiplication:

```python
import numpy as np

def knn(query: np.ndarray, vectors: np.ndarray, k: int = 5) -> np.ndarray:
    scores = vectors @ query                     # cosine similarity for unit vectors
    top = np.argpartition(-scores, k)[:k]        # unordered top-k, O(N)
    return top[np.argsort(-scores[top])]         # sort only the k winners
```

The cost grows linearly with N × dimensions. Do the maths for a realistic enterprise archive:

| Quantity | 1M chunks × 384 dims | 10M chunks × 1,536 dims |
|---|---|---|
| Memory (float32 = 4 bytes) | 1.5 GB | **61 GB** |
| Multiply-adds per query | 384 million | **15.4 billion** |
| Feels like | Fine on one machine | Too slow and too big for interactive use |

:::tip[Brute force is underrated]
Below roughly a few hundred thousand vectors, exact search with a good numeric library is often **fast enough, perfectly accurate, and has zero index to maintain**. Many internal tools and FDE pilots never need more. Measure before adding an approximate index.
:::

## The curse of dimensionality

High-dimensional space behaves strangely. As dimensions grow, randomly scattered points become **almost equally far from each other**: the gap between the nearest and the farthest neighbour shrinks relative to the distances themselves. "Nearest" stops meaning much.

Why does vector search work at all, then? Because **real embeddings aren't random**. They sit on lower-dimensional structures (topics, clusters), so meaningful neighbours really are much closer than everything else. Approximate indexes exploit that structure.

You can see this directly. Here's one run of the benchmark from this module's lab, with the same index settings on two kinds of data (a cloud container, numbers will differ on your machine):

| Data (384 dims) | HNSW recall@10 at `ef_search=40` |
|---|---|
| 200K vectors of uniform random noise | **0.03** (barely better than guessing) |
| 100K clustered vectors, like real embeddings | **1.00** |

Same algorithm, same parameters. The data's structure made all the difference. That's also a warning: **always benchmark on your real embeddings**, not synthetic ones.

## Memory math you'll need in customer conversations

Vectors usually dominate the RAM of a vector search system. Options to shrink them:

| Representation | Bytes per dimension | 10M × 1,536 | Typical quality impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| float32 | 4 | 61 GB | Baseline |
| float16 / bfloat16 ("halfvec") | 2 | 31 GB | Usually negligible |
| int8 scalar quantization | 1 | 15 GB | Small; often recovered by re-scoring top results with full vectors |
| Binary (1 bit) | 1/8 | 1.9 GB | Large on its own; used as a fast first pass, then re-scored |
| Fewer dimensions (if the model supports shortening) | — | e.g. 512 dims → 20 GB | Model-dependent; measure |

<ThinkFirst question="A customer has 40 million support-ticket chunks and wants semantic search with p95 under 200 ms, on infrastructure they control. Their embedding model produces 1,024-dim float32 vectors. Do the memory maths and propose a design.">

**Memory:** 40M × 1,024 × 4 bytes ≈ **164 GB** of raw float32 vectors, before any index overhead. That's too much for one ordinary node.

**Options to combine:**

1. **Quantize:** halfvec → about 82 GB; int8 → about 41 GB. Re-score the top candidates with full precision (stored on disk or in a separate column).
2. **Reduce dimensions** if the model supports shortened embeddings (e.g. 512 dims halves everything again). Validate recall on their data.
3. **Partition by tenant or time:** if most searches are scoped (one customer, last 2 years), you search a much smaller slice. Partitioning is often the biggest win.
4. **Approximate index (HNSW)** on the compressed vectors, sized to fit in RAM, possibly sharded across nodes.
5. **Measure:** build a retrieval eval and a load test before committing. "p95 < 200 ms" must hold at their concurrency, not just for one query.

A good FDE answer also asks: **do they really need all 40 million?** Old, duplicate or closed tickets may be archivable, the cheapest optimization of all.

</ThinkFirst>
